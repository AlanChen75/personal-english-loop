Q1. How did you calculate the MCC values and the main conclusion? Answer. On the sealed test data, we calculate MCC from each device's on/off confusion matrix, then average the nine device MCCs equally. The threshold-only baseline scores 0.8059; bounded LHS scores 0.6244 in the frozen seed 101 comparison. So our conclusion concerns the role of bounds in this comparison, not an overall win for the generator.

Q2. How did you get 2.22 percent, 6 percent, or the 0.3150 advantage? Answer. The compliance percentages are counts of complete configurations: 5/225 for LLM, and 54/900 for LHS. The 0.3150 is different: unbounded LLM-minus-LHS selected MCC on validation data, weighted equally by device. With equal dataset weighting, it becomes 0.2786. That post-hoc analysis adds no new test result.

Q3. Why do the bounded results seem contradictory? Answer. They answer different questions. In the single frozen seed 101 test, both bounded arms scored higher. Across 5 LLM test seeds, bounds lowered mean MCC from 0.6262 to 0.5951, while reducing variation. The validation pool audit is a third, post-hoc analysis. I would therefore not say “bounds always help”; the result depends on which comparison we report.

Q4. What exactly is the future experiment? Answer. We would use an independent controlled system with device states, physical behavior, and a feasible region specified before candidate generation. We would test correct, boundary-adjacent, and misspecified bounds, while varying candidate restrictions and reconstruction caps separately. That would distinguish the mechanisms and show where the approach fails. This validation is future work, not a completed experiment in this paper.
