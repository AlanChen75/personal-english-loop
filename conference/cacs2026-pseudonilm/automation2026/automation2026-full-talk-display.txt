Good morning. I am Chung-Kuang Chen from National Taiwan University of Science and Technology. In industrial non-intrusive load monitoring, calibration labels are often scarce. We ask whether an LLM proposes more useful calibration candidates than structured sampling under a small evaluation budget, and how engineering bounds change the answer. Our result is conditional: the relative advantage depends on bounds and on how performance is summarized.

Industrial NILM estimates individual device states from an aggregate power trace. In a new site, device-level labels may be unavailable during calibration. We therefore have to select parameters using aggregate data alone. Those parameters control such things as power thresholds and the reconstruction of each device's signal. A configuration that looks plausible by engineering intuition can still perform poorly on labeled device states. This makes candidate generation and candidate selection separate problems. Our experiment keeps the selection rule fixed and compares two ways to supply it with candidate configurations.

We used 3 industrial corpora: IMDELD, Case 1, and WELTRON, with 9 devices in total. Each trace was aligned to 1 power sample per second and divided chronologically into calibration, validation, and test partitions, in a 50%, 20%, 30% split. We crossed two candidate sources, one-shot LLM proposals and Latin hypercube sampling, with bounds enabled or disabled. For each dataset, source, bounds condition, and seed, the pool contained 15 joint configurations. The LLM's device proposals were aligned by index; we did not form every possible combination. Both sources received the same candidate-count and calibration-sample budgets.

The two sources do not receive identical generation information. The LLM sees device descriptions, rated power, aggregate statistics, parameter ranges, and the scorer description. The sampler uses the ranges and a seed. Once the candidates exist, however, the same deterministic scorer ranks every pool using aggregate power only. It combines residual fit, event agreement, state consistency, and separation, with weights of 0.35, 0.30, 0.20, and 0.15. No device-level test labels enter selection. We lock the chosen configuration before evaluating it against test labels. This design compares one-shot candidate sources under a common selector; it does not match generation time, cost, or information.

Here, a bound is a prescribed engineering range for a calibration parameter, such as a power factor or an on-ratio. In the bounded sampling arm, Latin hypercube sampling draws directly within the declared interval. It does not draw a wide value and then clip or reject it. The unbounded arm keeps its wider-range candidates. Bounds also restrict accepted events and cap reconstructed device power. So enabling bounds changes both the candidate domain and the output reconstruction rule. If performance changes, this experiment cannot assign the change to either part alone. Also, our declared rectangular ranges are not a measured ground-truth feasible region.

Our primary outcome is load-state detection, not energy attribution. For each of the 9 devices, we compare its predicted on and off states with test labels and count true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. From those 4 counts we calculate the Matthews correlation coefficient, or MCC. We then take the simple arithmetic mean of the 9 device MCC values. Each device receives equal weight, regardless of its duration or power rating. Event F-one is secondary. This distinction matters: the main number is not a single pooled confusion matrix, and it is not the aggregate-only score used to pick the configuration. The test labels are used only after selection is locked.

In the frozen seed 101 test comparison, LHS rose from 0.0991 without bounds to 0.6244 with bounds. The LLM arm rose from 0.4850 to 0.5577. Those are differences between complete bounded and unbounded procedures, not isolated effects of the candidate limits. The strongest pooled result was actually a simple threshold-only baseline, at 0.8059 MCC. This is important to state plainly: our more elaborate candidate generators did not win the overall test comparison. The observed gain in the bounded arms supports examining the role of engineering limits, while the baseline keeps the performance claim in perspective.

A single seed can be misleading, so we also have 5 LLM generation seeds with sealed-test results. Without bounds, mean MCC was 0.6262, with a sample standard deviation of 0.1131. With bounds, the mean was 0.5951, with a smaller standard deviation of 0.0413. In this 5-seed set, bounds reduced dispersion but did not increase mean performance. The LHS test result on the previous slide comes from 1 fixed seed. Its 20-seed repetition was examined only on validation data. We therefore should not describe the 5-seed LLM test comparison as a matched repeated-test contest against LHS.

We also asked whether wide-range proposals satisfy all declared active engineering limits. Only 5 out of 225 unbounded LLM joint configurations did so: 2.22%. For LHS, it was 54 out of 900, or 6%. The LLM proposals had a smaller average normalized violation distance, so they tended to lie closer to the ranges, but that did not translate into reliable full-configuration compliance. Neither source's selected unbounded configurations were fully compliant in this audit. “Close to a bound” and “satisfies every bound” are different claims. These feasibility figures come from an exploratory validation-only audit, not from additional sealed-test evaluations.

To understand selection, we examined existing validation pools after the main experiment. In the unbounded condition, selected LLM configurations exceeded selected LHS configurations by 0.3150 MCC when devices were weighted equally. When datasets were weighted equally, the difference was 0.2786. We separated two ingredients: the best validation MCC available inside each fixed pool, which is an oracle ceiling, and the gap between that ceiling and what our aggregate-only scorer selected, which is selection regret. Selected performance equals ceiling minus regret. The device-balanced decomposition suggested differences in both availability and selection; under dataset weighting, the oracle difference was close to zero and the regret difference dominated. These are post-hoc validation diagnostics. The oracle sees device labels and is not a deployable selector.

The evidence says that candidate feasibility and selection quality must be checked before claiming value from a more complex generator. On these data, the unbounded LLM pools had a selected validation advantage, but no bounded LLM advantage was detected. A simple threshold baseline remained strongest in the frozen pooled test result. We cannot say that the LLM learned physical laws, that bounds alone caused a gain, or that the method is ready for another facility. The bounds affect both generation and reconstruction, the two generators receive different information, and the repeated-test evidence is asymmetric. Those limits determine what the next experiment needs to isolate.

The next step is independent controlled validation. We would specify device states, physical behavior, and a feasible region independently of the generators. Then we would test correct, boundary-adjacent, and misspecified bounds while separating candidate restrictions from reconstruction caps. That would show when bounds help, when generation adds value, and when the procedure fails. For now, assess bounds, the selector, and a simple baseline together.
