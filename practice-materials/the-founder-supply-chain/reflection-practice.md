## Source reflection

The Founder shows how Ray Kroc moved from being a restaurant equipment salesman to building McDonald’s into a large restaurant chain. What interested me most was not simply his entrepreneurial success, but how he kept identifying new bottlenecks and changing the way he thought about the business. From automation and process standardization to franchising and later the real-estate model, the movie shows how a business can gradually become scalable.

At the beginning, Kroc sells the Multimixer, which can make several milkshakes at the same time. This represents the value of automation: one task can be completed faster with less labor. However, faster equipment does not automatically make the whole system efficient. The movie shows Kroc waiting at a traditional drive-in restaurant, where service is slow, the environment is confusing, and orders can be wrong. This scene made me realize that improving only one activity does not solve problems in ordering, preparation, delivery, and coordination.

This is why Kroc is so surprised when he first visits the McDonald brothers’ restaurant. He orders a hamburger, fries, and a drink and receives them almost immediately. He then looks for plates and utensils, only to learn that the food is served in paper packaging and can be eaten anywhere. The McDonald brothers did not simply ask employees to work faster. They reduced the menu, redesigned the kitchen layout, divided work into specific tasks, and standardized the preparation process. This improved speed, training, quality control, and cost control at the same time.

Another scene that impressed me was the difficulty of changing customer habits. At first, many customers stayed in their cars and honked their horns, expecting a carhop to serve them. Some were unhappy that they had to walk to the counter, eat from paper packaging, and throw away their own trash. The brothers even talked about the customer “learning curve.” Later, a boy comes to buy a bag of hamburgers, more cars arrive, and a line begins to form. This shows that process innovation can change not only internal operations but also customer expectations about speed, convenience, price, and service.

Once the process could be repeated reliably, franchising became much easier. If every restaurant depended on a different chef or different working methods, it would be difficult to expand. When products, equipment, work procedures, and quality standards are consistent, one successful store can be copied to many locations. As the number of stores grows, purchasing volume, common specifications, training, and brand recognition can create economies of scale. In my view, this is the real strength of McDonald’s: it developed a system that could be repeated, not just a successful product.

The milkshake dispute later in the movie also shows that supply chain management is not only about minimizing cost. As the franchise system grows, refrigeration equipment and electricity become important expenses, so Kroc wants to use powdered milkshake products. The McDonald brothers, however, believe that this would reduce quality. The decision becomes a trade-off among cost, efficiency, and product standards. Later, Kroc also realizes that franchise royalties alone do not give him enough profit or control, so the business moves toward a real-estate model. Each stage represents a new solution to a new limitation.

My main lesson from The Founder is that business competitiveness is built in layers. Automation improves individual tasks. Standardization improves the whole process. Standardization makes replication possible, and replication creates economies of scale. Larger scale then creates new cost and management problems that require another business model. McDonald’s competitive advantage is therefore not only its hamburgers or brand, but the operating system behind them: a system that can be repeated, controlled, measured, and continuously improved. This is the most valuable connection I found between the movie and our supply chain course.

## Simple speaking summary

The Founder is not only a story about McDonald’s. For me, it is a story about improving a business system.

At first, Ray Kroc sells a machine that makes milkshakes faster. But one fast machine cannot fix a slow process.

The McDonald brothers redesign the whole restaurant. They use a smaller menu, a better kitchen layout, clear tasks, and standard work. This makes the restaurant faster and easier to copy.

Standardization makes franchising possible. More stores create economies of scale, but larger scale also creates new problems.

My main lesson is simple: a strong business is not only about a good product. It also needs a system that can be repeated, measured, controlled, and improved.
